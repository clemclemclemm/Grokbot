Robots moved goods, not welds

The decision

Treat aisle time and high-risk routine inspection as CapEx inputs this quarter, not culture. Measure one unit before you buy or rent another cell.

What changed

Three Phil-green signals from IFR calendar year 2024 (World Robotics 2025 Service Robots). Survey coverage: 944 service-robot producers, 333 in logistics (not a world census):

Brief 1 · Indoor logistics. Professional service robots for transportation and logistics reached 102,900 units sold in 2024, up 14% (IFR). Professional service sales were more than 199,000 that year. Logistics took more than every other professional application in that survey window. IFR service figures are survey-based, not a census.

Brief 2 · RaaS. The Robot-as-a-Service fleet grew 31% in 2024 to more than 24,500 units (IFR). Our reading: the transport/logistics RaaS channel alone grew 42% that year, not all RaaS.

Brief 3 · Inspection. Inspection and maintenance professional service robots reached close to 2,800 units in 2024, up 2,476% from a tiny 2023 base (IFR; implied prior-year order ~100 units).

Why it matters

CapEx rooms still picture "robots" as caged arms. Unit volume already sits in goods movement. Waiting for a quiet CapEx year answers ownership while fleets under contract scale. Plants that still send people into confined spaces for routine checks sit on the wrong side of a niche curve that just steepened.

PME boss, plant chef, and fund share one ask: name the bottleneck in meters, waits, or inspection rounds before ownership.

What could make this wrong

One limit: close to 2,800 is the 2024 level, not the tiny base. The 2,476% rise is from a small 2023 denominator (implied ~100 units). A spike that steep from a niche can reverse. Treat it as direction against the people-default for high-risk routine checks, not a settled mass market.

Three questions for the room

1.Which meters of indoor travel or forklift waits are still human by default, and who owns that series?
2.Which bottleneck would you automate tomorrow if the balance sheet stayed light (buy, rent, or redesign)?
3.Which inspection rounds are high-frequency, low-skill, and high-risk enough to try mobile sensing before another production robot?

Recommended next move (90 days)

1.Days 1-3: name one unit (aisle meters, dock waits, or high-risk routine inspection) and one owner who already lives that work.
2.Days 4-30: collect a baseline finance can restate the same way every shift.
3.Days 31-60: one-page note; size buy, RaaS capacity, or flow redesign to the baseline only.
4.Days 61-90: run or refuse the sized response; no second unit before the first baseline exists.

Short reportage

Indoor logistics already takes the volume seat in the IFR 2024 survey window. Start plant tours at goods-in and WIP aisles, not only at the CNC. If the board never measures meters walked or line-side starvation, it is blind to the category that already led professional unit sales.

RaaS separates bottleneck from ownership. A light balance sheet does not excuse a missing unit. Prefer unit named, window dated, owner named, ask sized to evidence. Exit clauses, uptime, and data ownership are the live contract questions.

Inspection belongs only as candidates: frequent, dangerous routine checks. Horizon demos sit on the web brief.
