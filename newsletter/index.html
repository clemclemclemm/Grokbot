Most professional robots sold last year never touched a weld

They moved pallets, totes, and WIP indoors.

If your CapEx pack still opens at the CNC and skips the aisle, you are budgeting against the wrong picture of where robot volume already went.

For Australian plant leaders who own CapEx and floor control, and still lose hours to unnamed aisle time, inspection rounds, and data gaps.

102,900 Indoor logistics already won the unit race

Problem

CapEx rooms still treat "robots" as caged arms at the cell.

Flawed fix

Automate production first; leave forklift hours and WIP walking as "just labour."

Why it fails

Professional service robots for transportation and logistics reached 102,900 units sold in 2024, up 14% (IFR, 2024). IFR put professional sales at more than 199,000. Derived: 102,900 ÷ >199,000 ≈ 52%. About every second professional service robot sold moved goods indoors. IFR service figures are survey-based, not a census, so treat ~52% as majority order of magnitude.

Better move

Start plant tours and continuous-improvement boards at goods-in and WIP aisles. Treat aisle meters and forklift waits as process variables.

Action today

Pick one aisle or dock and write last week's starve or wait minutes the same way every shift. The quiet giant is aisle time, not another weld-cell brochure.

Sources IFR World Robotics 2025 Service Robots press release, 7 Oct 2025 (calendar year 2024 unit sales).

+31% RaaS fleets are outrunning many purchases

Problem

Boards block automation to protect a quiet CapEx year and avoid stranded metal.

Flawed fix

Wait until the balance sheet "has room," then buy a cell outright or do nothing.

Why it fails

The Robot-as-a-Service fleet grew 31% in 2024 to more than 24,500 units (IFR, 2024). The +42% figure is the transport/logistics RaaS channel only, not all RaaS. Finance rarely separates fleet-under-contract from CapEx buys, so the room argues last decade's ownership question.

Better move

Separate the bottleneck decision from the ownership decision. Measure first; then buy, rent, or redesign flow.

Action today

Name one bottleneck you would automate tomorrow if the balance sheet stayed light, and write the exit rule you would need. Fear the missing unit, not only the metal.

Sources IFR World Robotics 2025 Service Robots Executive Summary (RaaS fleet and channel, calendar year 2024).

+2,476% Inspection robots went vertical

Problem

Routine inspection still defaults to a person with a torch in dirty or dangerous places.

Flawed fix

Keep sending people into confined spaces, onto roofs, or along conveyors because "it has always worked," or wait for a big production-robot CapEx.

Why it fails

Inspection and maintenance professional service robots reached close to 2,800 units in 2024, up 2,476% (IFR, 2024). Sensing is entering high-frequency checks. Absolute units are still a tiny base; a spike this steep can reverse. The direction still prices against the people-default for routine risk.

Better move

List inspections that are high-frequency, low-skill, and high-risk. Those are mobile-sensing candidates before another production robot.

Action today

Circle three inspection rounds that put people into risk for routine work, and note frequency. Boring inspection is where autonomy is already moving.

Sources IFR World Robotics 2025 Service Robots Executive Summary, inspection and maintenance series (2024 vs 2023).

+142% Australian AI R&D spend more than doubled

Problem

Plants confuse tool adoption with research-backed capability.

Flawed fix

Buy licences and call it AI strategy, without sensors, labels, or stable cycle data.

Why it fails

Australian Business Expenditure on R&D (BERD) coded to artificial intelligence as a field of research rose from A$276 million in FY 2021-22 to A$668 million in FY 2023-24, up 142% (ABS, FY 2023-24). BERD measures funded research effort; adoption surveys measure who clicked a tool. FoR coding is not factory AI only, and the survey is biennial, but the dollar surge is real. Without legible process data you become a customer of other people's models.

Better move

Instrument the process before renting novelty. Make cycle data and night-shift definitions restatable.

Action today

Ask whether last month's scrap or starve minutes could be restated by someone who was not in the meeting. Capability follows data, not slogan fluency.

Sources ABS, Research and Experimental Development, Businesses, Australia, 2023-24 (field of research: artificial intelligence).

−3% Manufacturing is losing the R&D purpose race

Problem

National manufacturing ambition can mask where business R&D dollars actually point.

Flawed fix

Wait for the mood to "come back to factories" before wiring the plant.

Why it fails

BERD by socio-economic objective coded to manufacturing fell from A$4,800 million to A$4,644 million ( -3%) between FY 2021-22 and FY 2023-24 (ABS). Information and communication services SEO rose from A$3,915 million to A$5,891 million ( +50%). Derived: ICT-services SEO is about 1.27× manufacturing SEO ( 5,891 ÷ 4,644 ≈ 1.27). Total BERD rose 18% to A$24.4 billion while manufacturing-purpose dollars slipped. SEO is not always the industry performing the R&D; the reallocation signal still stands. Uninstrumented plants become customers of other people's research.

Better move

Show up in the capability stack by measuring process, not by quoting policy language.

Action today

Put one process series (starve, scrap, or inspection escapes) on the leadership agenda with an owner and a date. Ambition is not a labelled dataset.

Sources ABS BERD 2023-24 socio-economic objective tables; ABS media release on business R&D and AI (FY 2021-22 vs FY 2023-24).

4 t Forklifts that see pallet shift

Problem

Yard and goods-in chaos still assume square floors and perfect pallets.

Flawed fix

Buy a logistics demo that works on a clean show floor, or wait for humanoid theatre.

Why it fails

Hyundai Wia has shown a fully autonomous forklift using lidar, vision, safety scanners, and a digital twin, payload up to 4 tonnes, speed up to 6.5 km/h (Korea Times, Sep 2026). Ops are planned for May 2027 at Kia AutoLand Hwaseong, announced rather than proven production. Coverage stresses loading when trucks sink and floors are uneven.

Better move

Ask vendors which failure modes they handle when the world is not square. That sorts toys from tools.

Action today

Name one dock or aisle failure mode that still has no written definition. Horizon announcements widen the dock menu; they do not replace measuring aisle time this quarter.

Sources The Korea Times, 7 Sep 2026, Hyundai Wia autonomous forklift report.

This week's take · ~9 min

Name the aisle, then the capital

Goods movement is the quiet majority

Problem

CapEx packs still open on weld cells while the unit race sits in the aisle.

Indoor logistics already takes a majority share of professional service robots. Every second professional unit sold in 2024 moved stuff. Start plant tours at goods-in and WIP aisles, not only at the CNC. If the board never measures meters walked, forklift waits, or line-side starvation, the plant is blind to the category that already won unit share. Caged arms still matter. They are not the whole sales story.

RaaS sharpens finance. Fleets under contract grew 31% to more than 24,500 units; the transport/logistics RaaS channel alone grew 42%. Boards protecting a quiet CapEx year answer yesterday's ownership question. The live question is which bottleneck would move if capacity could scale without stranding metal.

Flawed fix

Automate production first. Treat aisle time as culture. Wait to buy metal outright, or do nothing until the CapEx year looks quiet.

Why it fails

Category confusion produces the wrong vetoes. "Robots" means a caged arm when volume is goods movement. "Budget" means heavy CapEx when fleets under contract are a parallel path. "AI" means a chatbot licence when research dollars need process data. "Not big enough for robots" when the bottleneck is meters walked. "Cannot afford CapEx" when capacity could be rented with an exit. "Already use AI" when scrap, inspection, and aisle starvation never entered the tick-box.

Better move

Fix the category, then pick the unit. Aisle time is a process variable. Ownership is a second decision after measurement. Waiting for a calmer year while logistics robots compound elsewhere is still a competitive choice, even when those robots are not welding cells.

High-frequency, low-skill, high-risk checks are inspection candidates; the people-default is already being priced against. Autonomous forklifts that lock pallet pose on uneven docks are announced for volume logistics, with ops planned for May 2027 and not yet proven in production. That widens what goods-in can look like. It does not replace measuring aisle time this quarter. Tours that never stop in the aisle miss where tempo dies: forklift waits, WIP walking, line-side starvation.

Australia funds AI research while manufacturing-purpose R&D slips

Australian Business Expenditure on R&D (BERD) coded to AI jumped 142%, from A$276 million to A$668 million (FY 2021-22 to FY 2023-24). BERD tracks funded research effort; adoption surveys track reported use. Confusing them softens capability talk.

Manufacturing SEO BERD fell 3% while ICT-services SEO rose 50% and now sits about 1.27× manufacturing SEO. Total business R&D rose as manufacturing-purpose dollars slipped. Intent is tilting.

Flawed fix

Borrow national industrial ambition as a substitute for plant instrumentation. Wait for the mood to "come back to factories" before wiring sensors. Buy licences and call it strategy.

Why it fails

Mood is not a MES. Ambition is not a labelled dataset. Without sensors, labels, and stable cycle data, plants become customers of other people's research: licences arrive, scrap stays flat. Compare two factories with the same headcount. One can restate starve minutes, inspection escapes, and dock waits by shift. The other can restate software licences and a vague Industry 4.0 roadmap. Only one can have an adult RaaS or inspection conversation. The other buys demos.

Better move

Instrument the process before you rent novelty. Keep labels honest: SEO is intended outcome, not always the industry doing the work; field-of-research AI is not "factory AI only"; soft engineering spend still dwarfs the AI field in absolute terms. None of those limits erase the direction. If cycle data is messy, night-shift variance is unnamed, and inspection still means a clipboard, the R&D wave will pass over the plant as a customer story, not a capability story.

One decision on the floor

Problem

Theme packs expand. Floors need one owned series.

Pick one unit for thirty to sixty days. Honest candidates: aisle meters still human by default, forklift waits that starve a cell, high-frequency inspections that send people into risk for routine checks. Name an owner who already lives that work. Collect a baseline finance can restate. Only then size mobile robots, RaaS, sensing, or vision-guided logistics to that baseline. No owner, no baseline, no pilot. No vendor-owned IP by default.

Flawed fix

Five simultaneous transformation workstreams. Pilots whose success rule is "we learned a lot." AI spend that cannot point at the same unit. Country or vendor slides as substitutes for a one-page baseline. A vendor demo before week six of a series.

Why it fails

Programmes expand. Owners compress. A steering pack can absorb every theme in one deck and still touch no aisle. An owner of forklift waits on Dock 2 faces last week's minutes. A light balance sheet does not excuse a missing unit. Renting without a dated series is theatre with an opex line instead of CapEx.

Better move

Prefer boring packs either way: unit named, window dated, owner named, ask sized to evidence. That sequence matters more under RaaS, not less. Exit clauses, service levels, uptime credits, and data ownership become the contract questions that used to hide inside CapEx depreciation debates. Fear of owning metal is rational when demand is lumpy. Fear of measuring the bottleneck is not. Measurement is cheap compared with another year of unnamed forklift overtime. If the baseline shows the pain is real, then ownership is a second decision: buy, rent, or redesign the flow so the meters disappear. Skipping measurement to avoid the second decision is how plants stay busy and behind. For Australian manufacturers that fear stranded assets after bumpy demand years, that distinction is the whole offer. Fear the missing unit, not the metal.

Worked example

Suppose line-side starvation from slow tote moves is the unit. Week one: define the starve minutes the same way every shift, name the owner, write what "robot" means in this pack so gadget language cannot sneak in. Weeks two to five: collect the series with people who live the aisle. Week six: one page for finance. Only then compare outright mobile robots, RaaS capacity, or process changes that remove travel. If a vendor demo happens before week six, it is colour, not a gate. The same template works for inspection rounds that send people into confined spaces, or for dock loading failures when floors are not square. Global unit shares and R&D pivots explain why waiting is costly. They do not replace the local series.

Use ninety days to prove seriousness without becoming a programme: unit and owner in the first three days, baseline through day thirty, finance note by day sixty, sized response by day ninety. If someone asks for a second unit before the first baseline exists, refuse. Selectivity matters when unnamed aisle hours stay invisible. Teams learn what leadership rewards. If leadership rewards slide volume, aisle time stays folk knowledge. If leadership rewards a dated aisle series that moves, the plant produces movement.

This week on the floor

Action this week

Name the unit and the date. Goods movement or high-risk routine inspection if those pains are real. Run the thirty-to-sixty-day baseline with people on the line. Process first if the number dies; mobile capacity or sensing if it holds. If plans assume last year's plant, change the pack.

Horizon tech belongs after the unit. Forklifts that see pallet shift expand docks by 2027 ; they do not excuse skipping aisle-time ownership this month. Tempo is local, owned, and dated. Early packs create commitments baselines cannot support. Pick the unit. Date it. Own it.

Opinion: the competitive risk this week is not missing a humanoid keynote; it is running unnamed aisle time and unnamed inspections while logistics robots already take majority professional share, RaaS fleets scale, inspection autonomy spikes, and Australian AI R&D dollars surge past manufacturing-purpose research. Name the unit. Own the baseline. Sequence the capital or the contract. Everything else is theatre.
