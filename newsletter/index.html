Most professional robots sold last year never touched a weld

They moved pallets, totes, and WIP indoors.

If your CapEx pack still opens at the CNC and skips the aisle, you are budgeting against the wrong picture of where robot volume already went.

102,900 Indoor logistics already won the unit race

Professional service robots for transportation and logistics reached 102,900 units sold in 2024, up 14% year-on-year (IFR, calendar year 2024).

IFR put professional service robot sales at more than 199,000 units that year. Derived share: 102,900 ÷ >199,000 ≈ 52% (majority order of magnitude; about 51.7% if the floor is taken as 199,000). About every second professional service robot sold was for moving goods, mostly indoors without public traffic.

This is not a humanoid story. It is pallet, tote, and line-side material flow. Mid-market CapEx rooms still equate "robots" with caged arms. The quiet giant is aisle time.

If forklift hours and WIP walking are still treated as "just labour," the plant is budgeting against a global market that already standardised mobile goods movement. Ask which meters of travel inside the building are still human by default. Treat aisle time as a process variable, not culture, remembering IFR service figures are survey-based, not a census, so the ~52% share is majority order of magnitude rather than a false-precise certificate.

Sources IFR World Robotics 2025 Service Robots press release, 7 Oct 2025 (calendar year 2024 unit sales).

+31% RaaS fleets are outrunning many purchases

The Robot-as-a-Service fleet grew 31% in 2024 to more than 24,500 units (IFR, 2024). The +42% figure is the transport/logistics RaaS channel only, not all RaaS, in the same year.

Flexible robotics contracts are scaling faster than many outright robot purchases. Finance rarely separates "fleet under contract" from CapEx robot buys. That separation matters.

Mid-market boards often block automation to keep a quiet CapEx year. RaaS exists so capacity can scale with demand without owning all the metal. The conversation shifts from "buy a cell" to "rent capacity with an exit." For manufacturers allergic to stranded assets, ask which bottleneck you would automate tomorrow if the balance sheet stayed light, keeping fleet size as more than 24,500 and +42% labelled to the transport/logistics RaaS channel only.

Sources IFR World Robotics 2025 Service Robots Executive Summary (RaaS fleet and channel, calendar year 2024).

+2,476% Inspection robots went vertical

Inspection and maintenance professional service robots reached close to 2,800 units sold in 2024, up 2,476% year-on-year (IFR, 2024).

That is a tiny base and an enormous growth rate. Sensing and autonomy are entering dirty and dangerous inspection work that used to default to a person with a torch.

Plants that still send people into confined spaces, onto roofs, or along conveyors for routine checks sit on the wrong side of a curve that just steepened from a niche. CapEx should ask which inspections are high-frequency, low-skill, and high-risk. Those are candidates for mobile sensing before another production robot, with the caveat that close to 2,800 is still a tiny base and a spike this steep can reverse.

Sources IFR World Robotics 2025 Service Robots Executive Summary, inspection and maintenance series (2024 vs 2023).

+142% Australian AI R&D spend more than doubled

Australian Business Expenditure on R&D (BERD) coded to artificial intelligence as a field of research rose from A$276 million in FY 2021-22 to A$668 million in FY 2023-24, up 142% (ABS, FY 2023-24).

Adoption surveys measure who clicked a tool. BERD measures who funded research effort. A$668 million into AI research fields means capability is being built in software and models, not only licences.

For a manufacturer, the CapEx question is whether process data is even legible to that stack. Without sensors, labels, and stable cycle data, you cannot buy your way into the R&D wave. You become a customer of other people's models, noting ABS field-of-research coding is not factory AI only and the survey is biennial.

Sources ABS, Research and Experimental Development, Businesses, Australia, 2023-24 (field of research: artificial intelligence).

−3% Manufacturing is losing the R&D purpose race

BERD by socio-economic objective coded to manufacturing fell from A$4,800 million in FY 2021-22 to A$4,644 million in FY 2023-24, down 3% (ABS). Information and communication services SEO rose from A$3,915 million to A$5,891 million, up 50%.

Derived: ICT-services SEO is now about 1.27× manufacturing SEO ( 5,891 ÷ 4,644 ≈ 1.27). Business R&D intent is tilting to information services while manufacturing-purpose dollars slip. Industry-of-performance manufacturing BERD also eased (A$5,203 million to A$5,047 million) while total BERD rose 18% to A$24.4 billion (same ABS release family).

"Future Made in Australia" talk sits beside an R&D purpose pivot. Mid-market plants that do not instrument processes will not show up cleanly in the AI field surge or in manufacturing SEO. They become customers of other people's R&D, not producers of it, remembering SEO is not the industry performing the R&D even while the reallocation signal stays clear.

Sources ABS BERD 2023-24 socio-economic objective tables; ABS media release on business R&D and AI (FY 2021-22 vs FY 2023-24).

4 t Forklifts that see pallet shift

Hyundai Wia has shown a fully autonomous forklift using lidar, vision, safety scanners, and a digital twin, with payload up to 4 tonnes and speed up to 6.5 km/h (Korea Times, Sep 2026). Production logistics start is planned for May 2027 at Kia AutoLand Hwaseong.

Coverage emphasises loading and unloading when trucks sink and floors are uneven: vision locks pallet pose, lidar maps the aisle. Unmanned forklifts that see pallet shift are announced for a dated volume plant, with ops planned for May 2027 rather than proven production today.

For plants with yard and goods-in chaos, ask vendors which failure modes they handle when the world is not square. That question sorts toys from tools. The local question is which dock, aisle, or pallet problem would still be unnamed if this class of system were common in peer plants by 2027, treating the Korea Times piece as an announcement with ops planned for May 2027, not proven production.

Sources The Korea Times, 7 Sep 2026, Hyundai Wia autonomous forklift report.

This week's take · ~11 min

Name the aisle, then the capital

Goods movement is the quiet majority

Professional service robotics is no longer a sideshow of novelty arms. Indoor logistics already takes a majority share of professional units. Every second professional service robot sold in 2024 was built to move stuff.

That should change plant tours. Start at goods-in and WIP aisles, not only at the CNC. If the continuous improvement board never measures meters walked, forklift waits, or line-side starvation, the plant is blind to the category that already won global unit share. Caged arms still matter. They are not the whole story of "robots" in 2024 sales.

RaaS sharpens the finance angle. Fleets under contract grew 31% to more than 24,500 units, and the transport/logistics RaaS channel alone grew 42%. Boards that stall automation to protect a quiet CapEx year are answering yesterday's ownership question. The live question is which bottleneck would move if capacity could scale without stranding metal on the balance sheet.

Aisle time is a process variable. Ownership model is a design choice. Waiting for a calmer year while logistics robots compound elsewhere is still a competitive choice, even when the robots in question are not welding cells.

Inspection robots make the sensing story concrete without fusion theatre. High-frequency, low-skill, high-risk checks are the candidates. Sending people into confined spaces or along conveyors for routine inspection is a default that the market is already pricing against.

Autonomous forklifts that lock pallet pose on uneven docks are announced for volume logistics, with ops planned for May 2027 and not yet proven in production. That expands possibles for goods-in. It does not replace measuring aisle time this quarter.

The practical failure mode in CapEx rooms is category confusion. Someone says "robots" and the room pictures a caged arm. The IFR service numbers say the volume story in professional units is goods movement. Someone says "automation budget" and the room pictures a heavy CapEx year. The RaaS numbers say fleets under contract are a parallel path. Someone says "AI" and the room pictures a chatbot licence. The BERD numbers say research dollars are being built into models and software stacks that need process data to matter on a floor.

Category confusion produces the wrong vetoes. "We are not a big enough plant for robots" when the bottleneck is meters walked. "We cannot afford CapEx this year" when the bottleneck could be rented capacity with an exit. "We already use AI" when the tick-box never touched scrap, inspection, or aisle starvation. Fix the category, then pick the unit.

Australia is funding AI research while manufacturing-purpose R&D slips

Australian Business Expenditure on R&D (BERD) coded to AI as a field jumped 142%, from A$276 million to A$668 million between FY 2021-22 and FY 2023-24. BERD tracks funded research effort. Adoption surveys track reported use. Confusing them is how rooms get soft about capability.

At the same time, BERD aimed at manufacturing as a socio-economic objective fell 3%, while information and communication services SEO rose 50%. ICT-services SEO is now about 1.27× manufacturing SEO ( 5,891 ÷ 4,644 ≈ 1.27) on those ABS figures. Total business R&D rose even as manufacturing-purpose dollars slipped. The country's R&D intent is tilting.

For a mid-market plant, the practical risk is becoming a pure customer of other people's research. AI field dollars and ICT-services SEO will show up as tools, models, and services sold into factories. Plants without sensors, labels, and stable cycle data cannot participate as producers of that stack. They can only buy outputs. Buying outputs without legible process data is how licences arrive and scrap stays flat.

There is a second Australian trap: borrowing national industrial ambition as a substitute for plant instrumentation. Policy language can sound manufacturing-heavy while BERD purpose dollars tilt to ICT services. A plant that waits for the national mood to "come back to factories" before wiring sensors will wait through the exact years when AI research spend compounds elsewhere. Mood is not a MES. Ambition is not a labelled dataset.

Compare two factories with the same headcount. One can restate starve minutes, inspection escapes, and dock waits by shift. The other can restate last quarter's software licences and a vague Industry 4.0 roadmap. Only one of those factories can have an adult conversation with a RaaS provider, a mobile robot integrator, or an inspection autonomy vendor. The other can only buy demos.

Keep the labels honest. SEO is intended outcome, not always the industry doing the work. Field-of-research AI is not "factory AI only." Soft engineering spend still dwarfs the AI field in absolute terms. None of those limits erase the direction: AI research funding surged; manufacturing-purpose R&D did not.

The CapEx implication is narrow. Instrument the process before you rent novelty. If cycle data is messy, night-shift variance is unnamed, and inspection still means a clipboard, the R&D wave will pass over the plant as a customer story, not a capability story.

One decision on the floor

The operating response is still one decision, not a theme pack.

Pick one unit for thirty to sixty days. For many plants this week, the honest candidates sit in goods movement and inspection: meters of aisle travel that are human by default, forklift waits that starve a cell, high-frequency inspections that send people into risk for routine checks. Name an owner who already lives that work. Collect a baseline finance can restate. Only then discuss mobile robots, RaaS capacity, sensing platforms, or vision-guided logistics, sized to the baseline. No owner, no baseline, no pilot. No vendor-owned IP by default.

That sequence matters more under RaaS, not less. A light balance sheet does not excuse a missing unit. Renting capacity without a dated series still produces theatre, only with an operating expense line instead of a CapEx line. Finance should prefer boring packs either way: unit named, window dated, owner named, ask sized to evidence.

Why one unit? Programmes expand. Owners compress. A steering pack can absorb logistics dominance, RaaS growth, inspection spikes, AI BERD, SEO pivots, and autonomous forklifts in one deck and still touch no aisle. An owner of forklift waits on Dock 2 has to face last week's minutes. Compression is the point.

Worked example. Suppose line-side starvation from slow tote moves is the unit. Week one: define the starve minutes the same way every shift, name the owner, write what "robot" means in this pack so gadget language cannot sneak in. Weeks two to five: collect the series with people who live the aisle. Week six: one page for finance. Only then compare outright mobile robots, RaaS capacity, or process changes that remove travel. If a vendor demo happens before week six, it is colour, not a gate.

The same template works for inspection rounds that send people into confined spaces, or for dock loading failures when floors are not square. Global unit shares and R&D pivots explain why waiting is costly. They do not replace the local series.

Refuse five simultaneous transformation workstreams. Refuse pilots whose success rule is "we learned a lot." Refuse AI spend that cannot point at the same unit. Refuse country or vendor slides as substitutes for the one-page baseline. Use ninety days to prove seriousness without letting the work become a programme: unit and owner in the first three days, baseline through day thirty, finance note by day sixty, sized response by day ninety. If someone asks for a second unit before the first baseline exists, refuse.

RaaS and mobile logistics also change what "stranded asset" fear should attach to. Fear of owning metal is rational when demand is lumpy. Fear of measuring the bottleneck is not rational. Measurement is cheap compared with another year of unnamed forklift overtime. If the baseline shows the pain is real, then the ownership model is a second decision: buy, rent, or redesign the flow so the meters disappear. Skipping measurement to avoid the second decision is how plants stay busy and behind.

RaaS does not remove governance. It changes where the governance sits. Exit clauses, service levels, uptime credits, and data ownership become the contract questions that used to hide inside CapEx depreciation debates. A light balance sheet with a vague success rule is still theatre. A light balance sheet with a dated starve-minute series is a grown-up operating model.

For Australian manufacturers that fear stranded assets after bumpy demand years, that distinction is the whole offer. Fear the missing unit, not the metal. Buy or rent after the unit exists.

High-risk routine checks are no longer an automatic people default in the niche that expanded. Ask which checks are frequent, standardised, and dangerous. Those are the candidates. Everything else can wait.

Selectivity matters when unnamed aisle hours stay invisible. Teams learn what leadership rewards. If leadership rewards slide volume, aisle time stays folk knowledge. If leadership rewards a dated aisle series that moves, the plant produces movement. The only question is whether unnamed aisle hours are part of someone else's advantage.

This week on the floor

Name the unit and the date. Goods movement and high-risk routine inspection are fair starting points if those pains are real. Run the thirty-to-sixty-day baseline with the people on the line. Then size the response: process first if the number dies under scrutiny, mobile capacity or sensing if the number holds. If plans still assume last year's plant, say so and change the pack.

Horizon tech belongs after the unit, not before. Forklifts that see pallet shift expand what docks can become by 2027. They do not excuse skipping aisle-time ownership this month. Tempo is local, owned, and dated. Brochures are optional. If a pack arrives before a series exists, the pack is early. Early packs create commitments baselines cannot support.

This week is still concrete. Pick the unit. Date it. Own it.

Opinion: the competitive risk this week is not missing a humanoid keynote; it is running unnamed aisle time and unnamed inspections while logistics robots already take majority professional share, RaaS fleets scale, inspection autonomy spikes, and Australian AI R&D dollars surge past manufacturing-purpose research. Name the unit. Own the baseline. Sequence the capital or the contract. Everything else is theatre.
